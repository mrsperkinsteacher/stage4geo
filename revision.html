Landscapes and Landforms

How landscapes and landforms are valued, changed, and managed - including a case study of a geomorphic hazard.

Key vocabulary

LandscapeThe big-picture view of an area - natural features (mountains, rivers) and human features (cities, farms) together.
LandformOne individual natural feature within a landscape, e.g. a mountain, valley, cliff or dune.
WeatheringRock breaking down in place - by water, wind or changes in temperature - without being moved.
ErosionRock or soil being picked up and moved by water, wind or ice.
DepositionEroded material being dropped and settled in a new location, building new landforms like beaches and dunes.
UrbanisationThe growth of cities and towns as more people move from rural areas to urban ones.
StakeholderA person or group with an interest in how a piece of land is used or changed.
CountryFor Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, land as identity and kin, not just a place - cared for, not owned.
SustainabilityUsing resources in a way that meets present needs without harming the future.
Real-world case study to revise with

Mount Keira / Djeera and the Illawarra escarpment

A real NSW landscape that maps neatly onto every chapter here, as a six-stage case study:

  1. An introduction to the Illawarra - the geomorphic processes that formed the coastal and escarpment landscape (Ch.1).
  2. The first people of the Illawarra - the aesthetic, cultural, spiritual and economic value of Mount Keira / Djeera to the Dharawal people (Ch.2).
  3. Competing interests - modern uses of the mountain and the conflicts between different groups' values, compared against Queenstown, Cairns and Hobart (Ch.2-3).
  4. The Illawarra is changing - a timeline of use since European colonisation, nicknamed "Red gold" (coal), "Black gold", "Adrenalin" (mountain biking) and "The future?" (Ch.3).
  5. Managing the escarpment - Dharawal management practices compared with the modern bodies responsible for the mountain today (Ch.4).
  6. Geomorphic hazards: landslides - the real 2012 Mount Keira landslide, used the same way the textbook uses Japan 2011 (Ch.5).

Good for students who want a local, close-to-home example to compare against the textbook's own case studies.

Learn about Dharawal Country - on Djeera (Mount Keira) as a significant women's mountain, linked to the Five Sisters dreaming story.

Key ideas to revise

Natural processes

  • Weathering - rock breaks down in place (water, wind, temperature).
  • Erosion - rock or soil is picked up and moved (rivers, waves, wind).
  • Deposition - material is dropped and settled, building new landforms.
  • Coastal example: weather the cliff → break off chunks → carry along the coast → deposit as a beach.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Human processes

  • Four big ones: farming, urbanisation, mining, tourism.
  • Human change is often far faster than natural change - a mine can remove a hill in months.
  • Weigh up: jobs, infrastructure, food security against pollution, habitat loss, soil degradation.
  • Case study to know: the Twelve Apostles (tourism) or Western Sydney's growth into the Cumberland Plain (urban sprawl).
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Perspectives on the land

  • Stakeholders see the same landscape differently: residents, tourists, environmental groups, government.
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective: land is Country - identity, not ownership - cared for through cultural burning, seasonal knowledge, totemic protection.
  • Modern conservation instead uses national parks, scientific monitoring and laws.
  • Land-use decision: identify stakeholders → consult perspectives → weigh pros and cons → decide.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Evaluating change

  • Judge every change against three pillars: environmental, economic, social. Rarely all good or all bad.
  • Case study to know: the Snowy Mountains Scheme - jobs and cheap power versus flooded valleys and displaced farming communities.
  • Method: identify the change → gather evidence → analyse all three impacts → judge whether it's sustainable.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Writing in geography

  • Describing a place: Location → Landscape.
  • Explaining a process: Process → Cause → Impact (e.g. "Wave erosion is shaping the coastline because powerful waves wear away limestone cliffs, causing sections to collapse into stacks.")
  • Writing a perspective fairly: Group → Viewpoint → Reason.
  • Report structure: Introduction → Body paragraphs → Perspectives → Conclusion (a judgement, not just a summary).
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Place and Liveability

What makes a place liveable, how perceptions of liveability differ, and what can be done to enhance it.

Key vocabulary

LiveabilityA measure of what a place is like to live in, based on a range of factors.
Factors affecting liveabilityThe reasons that make a place more or less enjoyable to live in - environmental, social, economic and political.
PerceptionA person's own view of how liveable a place is, shaped by their experiences, culture and what they value.
Access to servicesHow easily people can reach things they need, like healthcare, education and transport.
Environmental qualityHow clean, healthy and hazard-free an environment is - affected by pollution, land degradation and natural hazards.
Social connectednessThe relationships people have with others, and how strongly they feel part of a community.
InfrastructureThe facilities and services a community needs to function, e.g. roads, education and healthcare.
Enhancing liveabilityActions taken by individuals, community groups, councils or governments to make a place more liveable.
Urban sprawlThe spread of a city outward into new low-density suburbs, often far from jobs and services.
Natural surveillanceBeing able to see public spaces from homes, shops, streets and parks - a design feature that improves perceived safety.
Real-world case study to revise with

Gosford vs the Global Liveability Index

The same comparison used in class: our own local area against the Economist Intelligence Unit's Global Liveability Index, which scores 173 cities worldwide on stability, healthcare, culture and environment, education, and infrastructure (Ch.6).

The 2025 top 5: Copenhagen (1st), Vienna and Zurich (tied 2nd), Melbourne (4th), Geneva (5th). Two more Australian cities also made the top 10 - Sydney (6th) and Adelaide (9th).
Near the bottom: Damascus was ranked least liveable in 2025, with Tripoli, Dhaka, Karachi and Algiers also near the bottom of the index - cities affected by conflict or instability.

Good exam practice: pick one liveability factor (e.g. access to services, environmental quality, community/social connectedness) and compare how it plays out in Gosford versus one highly-ranked and one low-ranked city above.

Worth remembering too: a ranking like this only measures what it chooses to measure (the EIU uses 30 indicators). It says little about belonging, culture or spiritual wellbeing - good for a "how reliable is this data?" question (Ch.6.4-6.5).

Key ideas to revise

Influences and perceptions (Ch.6)

  • Environmental factors: climate, green space, pollution, natural hazards.
  • Human/social factors: safety, culture, community, income, services.
  • Two people can rate the same place very differently - liveability is partly personal.
  • Measuring liveability mixes quantitative data (crime rate, income) and qualitative data (survey opinions).
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Place and Liveability lessons

Access to services and facilities (Ch.7)

  • Key services: healthcare, education, transport, emergency services, green space.
  • Access depends on location - rural areas often travel much further for the same service than urban areas.
  • Limited access lowers liveability even when other factors (climate, safety) are strong.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Place and Liveability lessons

Environmental quality (Ch.8)

  • Reduced by pollution, land degradation, natural hazards and urban sprawl.
  • Case study: Mount Atkinson Estate, Truganina VIC, and the proposed Werribee satellite city, built to ease sprawl around growing outer-Melbourne suburbs.
  • Traffic congestion case study: Melbourne, where 83.9% of households own at least one car, compared with Malmo, Sweden (420km of cycle paths, a quarter of trips by bike).
  • Cities produce around three-quarters of the world's carbon dioxide emissions.
Threats to Liveability (class resource)

Community (Ch.9)

  • Social connectedness is built through shared spaces, local groups and feeling safe.
  • Perceived safety (how safe people feel) isn't always the same as actual safety (crime statistics).
  • Safer design: clear sightlines, buildings facing public spaces, natural surveillance (people can see the space) and formal surveillance (cameras, patrols).
  • Neighbourhood Watch enhances connectedness directly: know your neighbours, share information, build trust.
Liveability in Australia: Building Safe Communities (class resource)

Enhancing liveability (Ch.10)

  • Different groups play different roles: government (planning, infrastructure), NGOs (300 Blankets - supporting people experiencing homelessness), community groups, and individuals.
  • Planning strategy to know: the 15-Minute City - designing neighbourhoods so daily needs are a short walk, cycle or ride away.
  • Caring for Country: Aboriginal joint management of land across NSW improves liveability and environmental quality together.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Place and Liveability lessons

Real-world case study to revise with

The Murray-Darling Basin

Australia's own case study for the value of water (Ch.15) and water scarcity and management (Ch.14) - one river system, several very different values placed on the same water:

Economic value - the Basin produces around a third of Australia's food supply, with agriculture (especially irrigation) the biggest single user of its water.
Cultural/spiritual value - Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples' connection to the Basin's rivers and wetlands goes back tens of thousands of years, and water management today increasingly includes Aboriginal water rights and cultural flows (Ch.15.3).
Scarcity pressures - over-allocation between states, drought and climate change have all strained the system, making it a real example of competing water users having to be managed together (Ch.14.1-14.2).

Good to contrast against the textbook's other two water case studies: Mawsynram, India (the world's wettest place - too much water) and the Gordon Dam, Tasmania (water stored and managed for hydropower).
