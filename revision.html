Real-world case study to revise with

Mount Keira / Djeera and the Illawarra escarpment

A real NSW landscape that maps neatly onto every chapter here, taught by the Illawarra EEC as a six-lesson excursion program:

1. An introduction to the Illawarra - the geomorphic processes that formed the coastal and escarpment landscape (Ch.1).
2. The first people of the Illawarra - the aesthetic, cultural, spiritual and economic value of Mount Keira / Djeera to the Dharawal people (Ch.2).
3. Competing interests - modern uses of the mountain and the conflicts between different groups' values, compared against Queenstown, Cairns and Hobart (Ch.2-3).
4. The Illawarra is changing - a timeline of use since European colonisation, nicknamed "Red gold" (coal), "Black gold", "Adrenalin" (mountain biking) and "The future?" (Ch.3).
5. Managing the escarpment - Dharawal management practices compared with the modern bodies responsible for the mountain today (Ch.4).
6. Geomorphic hazards: landslides - the real 2012 Mount Keira landslide, used the same way the textbook uses Japan 2011 (Ch.5).

Good for students who want a local, close-to-home example to compare against the textbook's own case studies.

Real-world case study to revise with

Uluru-Kata Tjuta: whose decision is it to manage Country?

A second case study, useful for Ch.2 (cultural and spiritual value) and Ch.4 (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander use and management):

The landform - Uluru is a single slab of arkose sandstone rising 348 metres above the surrounding desert plain, changing colour at sunrise and sunset, and listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (Ch.1-2).
Cultural and spiritual value - Uluru is sacred to the Anangu people, its Traditional Owners. Tjukurpa - the Anangu law, stories and beliefs - connects specific features of the rock to creation stories, including the Mala story, the Kuniya and Liru story, and the Lungkata story (Ch.2).
Handback and joint management - the land was formally handed back to the Anangu people in 1985, and is now managed jointly between the Anangu people and Parks Australia (Ch.4).
The climbing ban - tourists had climbed Uluru since the 1930s, but on 26 October 2019 it was officially closed to climbers, with a warning sign raised at Mala Parking. The decision followed decades of requests from the Anangu people, for whom the climb crossed a sacred site (Ch.4).

Good for an exam question on who gets a say in how a landscape is managed, and what happens when tourism and cultural value come into conflict.

Real-world case study to revise with

Gosford vs the Global Liveability Index

The same comparison used in class: our own local area against the Economist Intelligence Unit's Global Liveability Index, which scores 173 cities worldwide on stability, healthcare, culture and environment, education, and infrastructure (Ch.6).

The 2025 top 5: Copenhagen (1st), Vienna and Zurich (tied 2nd), Melbourne (4th), Geneva (5th). Two more Australian cities also made the top 10 - Sydney (6th) and Adelaide (9th).
Near the bottom: Damascus was ranked least liveable in 2025, with Tripoli, Dhaka, Karachi and Algiers also near the bottom of the index - cities affected by conflict or instability.

Good exam practice: pick one liveability factor (e.g. access to services, environmental quality, community/social connectedness) and compare how it plays out in Gosford versus one highly-ranked and one low-ranked city above.

Worth remembering too: a ranking like this only measures what it chooses to measure (the EIU uses 30 indicators). It says little about belonging, culture or spiritual wellbeing - good for a "how reliable is this data?" question (Ch.6.4-6.5).

Real-world case study to revise with

The Murray-Darling Basin

Australia's own case study for the value of water (Ch.15) and water scarcity and management (Ch.14) - one river system, several very different values placed on the same water:

Economic value - the Basin produces around a third of Australia's food supply, with agriculture (especially irrigation) the biggest single user of its water.
Cultural/spiritual value - Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples' connection to the Basin's rivers and wetlands goes back tens of thousands of years, and water management today increasingly includes Aboriginal water rights and cultural flows (Ch.15.3).
Scarcity pressures - over-allocation between states, drought and climate change have all strained the system, making it a real example of competing water users having to be managed together (Ch.14.1-14.2).

Good to contrast against the textbook's other two water case studies: Mawsynram, India (the world's wettest place - too much water) and the Gordon Dam, Tasmania (water stored and managed for hydropower).
