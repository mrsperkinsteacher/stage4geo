One plain steel box made world trade faster and cheaper. Find out how, and why a few narrow sea passages matter so much.

📋 Summary of Lesson

Today you follow the story of the shipping container, from the first container ship in 1956 to the giant ships of today. You summarise a short video at three levels of detail, then find three famous chokepoints on a map: the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal and the Strait of Malacca. You finish with the 2021 Ever Given case study.

This matters because almost everything in a shop has travelled in a box. One standard size meant ships, cranes, trains and trucks could all be built around it, so goods moved faster and cost less. But busy routes squeeze through a few narrow places, so one blockage can slow trade across the world.

📖 Glossary

Shipping container - a strong steel box of a standard size that goods are packed into for transport.
Break-bulk - cargo loaded piece by piece, in sacks, crates and barrels, instead of in containers.
Freight - goods that are transported, usually in large amounts.
Shipping route - the path ships regularly follow between ports.
Chokepoint - a narrow passage on a trade route that many ships must pass through.
Canal - a waterway dug by people.
Strait - a narrow natural waterway joining two larger bodies of water.

💡 Did You Know?

About 1.9 million seafarers crew the world's ships. A 2024 report warned that shortages of seafarers had reached a 17-year high, a risk for world trade.

Source: Global Maritime Forum, 'Global supply chains at risk without seafarer reforms, new report urges', 10 September 2024.

📰 In the News

A drought hit the Panama Canal in 2023. October 2023 was the driest October on record there, and the lake that feeds the canal's locks ran low. For the first time in its history the canal had to limit ship crossings: normally about 36 ships a day, cut to 22, then lifted to 24 from January 2024.

Sources: Panama Canal Authority, 15 December 2023; US Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2 December 2024.

🗺️ Where in the World?

Strait of Malacca - the sea passage between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra that links the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea and the Pacific. At its narrowest point, near Singapore, it is only about 2.7 km (1.7 miles) wide. In 2016 about 16 million barrels of oil a day passed through it.

Sources: US Energy Information Administration, 11 August 2017.

💼 Careers Connected to This Lesson

Ship's officer - navigates and runs a cargo ship on long ocean routes, often through busy chokepoints like the Strait of Malacca.

Container crane operator - lifts boxes on and off ships at ports such as Port Botany. Because every box is the same size, one crane can handle them all.
