📋 Summary of Lesson

Today you work like a geographer with real data about Australia's macadamia trade. You draw a line graph of the crop from 2020 to 2024, a column graph of the biggest export markets, and a pie chart of how much of the crop is exported. Then you use all three graphs to describe the trade and name one influence on it.

This matters because different graphs answer different questions. A line graph shows change over time, a column graph compares categories and a pie chart shows parts of a whole. Choosing the right graph, and reading its scale carefully, is a skill every geographer needs.

📖 Glossary

Native - a plant or animal that comes from a place naturally.
Kernel - the part of the nut we eat, once the hard shell is removed.
Production - the amount of a product grown or made, often measured in tonnes.
Export destination - the country that goods are sold to.
Trend - the overall direction of data over time: rising, falling or steady.
Domestic market - buyers inside a product's own country.

💡 Did You Know?

Australia has about 15 million macadamia trees growing on 47,659 hectares, looked after by about 800 growers. Bundaberg in Queensland is the biggest growing region, and the Northern Rivers of New South Wales is second.

Source: Australian Macadamia Society, Facts and figures (as of May 2026).

📰 In the News

The 2025 macadamia crop was 46,940 tonnes (in the shell, at 10 per cent moisture), well below the 57,850 tonnes of 2024. The Australian Macadamia Society said heavy and prolonged rain damaged crops in every growing region and pushed the harvest into September and October. The outlook for 2026 is more hopeful, with strong flowering in most regions.

Source: Australian Macadamia Society, '2025 Australian macadamia crop finalised following challenging season', 2 December 2025.

🗺️ Where in the World?

Hawaii, USA - macadamias are native to Australia, but seeds were taken to Hawaii in the 1880s and planted as windbreaks for sugar cane. Hawaii's macadamia industry grew from the 1920s, and for a long time many people around the world called it the 'Hawaiian nut'. Australia only began farming it seriously in the 1960s.

Source: Australian Geographic (2017).
