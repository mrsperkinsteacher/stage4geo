📋 Summary of Lesson

First Nations Australian art - like paintings from remote community art centres - connects artists directly to buyers all over the world, bringing income, recognition and pride back to their communities. But there's a serious problem: many "Aboriginal-style" souvenirs sold in Australian shops are fakes, mass-produced overseas with no First Nations artist involved and no payment going back to Country. This lesson asks you to think about who is harmed by fake art, and what it means to be an ethical buyer.

📖 Glossary

Export - a good or service sold from one country to buyers in another country.
Authentic - genuinely made or licensed by the person or community it claims to come from.
Art centre - a community-owned organisation that supports artists, documents their work and sells it fairly on their behalf.
Royalty - a payment made to an artist when their work is sold or reproduced.
Regulator - a government body that makes and enforces rules to keep trade fair and honest.

💡 Did You Know?

The world-famous Western Desert art movement began in 1971 when a school teacher, Geoffrey Bardon, encouraged Aboriginal men at Papunya (NT) to paint a mural on a blank school wall. That led to Papunya Tula Artists (1972) - Australia's first entirely Aboriginal-owned and directed art company - whose work later showed in New York, London, Paris and Venice.

Source: National Museum of Australia; Smithsonian Magazine.

📰 In the News

14 December 2022: The Productivity Commission's Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Visual Arts and Crafts study found that around two in three "Aboriginal-style" souvenirs sold in Australia are not made or licensed by First Nations people, out of a genuine Indigenous visual arts market worth over $250 million a year. In 2019, the Federal Court fined the company Birubi Art $2.3 million for selling souvenirs made in Indonesia as "Authentic Aboriginal Art".

Source: ABC News, 14 December 2022; Productivity Commission Study Report.

2026: Following the Productivity Commission's findings, the Australian Government's Office for the Arts sought public feedback on new laws that would require clear labelling of Indigenous-style art and merchandise, so shoppers can tell real from fake at a glance.

Source: Australian Government, Office for the Arts, "Seeking Views on New Laws to Tackle Fake Indigenous-Style Art."

Ongoing: The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) has repeatedly warned that fake Aboriginal art "diminishes the authenticity of the whole industry" and can mislead shoppers under Australian Consumer Law - one reason the Indigenous Art Code exists to certify genuine sellers.

Source: ABC News, "Fake Aboriginal art diminishes authenticity of the whole industry, ACCC says."

🗺️ Where in the World?

Papunya, Northern Territory - a remote community roughly 240km north-west of Alice Springs, on Luritja/Pintupi Country. This is where the Western Desert art movement began and where Papunya Tula Artists was founded in 1972.
