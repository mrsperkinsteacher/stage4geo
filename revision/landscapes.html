Landscapes and Landforms

How landscapes and landforms are valued, changed, and managed - including a case study of a geomorphic hazard.

Key vocabulary

LandscapeThe big-picture view of an area - natural features (mountains, rivers) and human features (cities, farms) together.
LandformOne individual natural feature within a landscape, e.g. a mountain, valley, cliff or dune.
WeatheringRock breaking down in place - by water, wind or changes in temperature - without being moved.
ErosionRock or soil being picked up and moved by water, wind or ice.
DepositionEroded material being dropped and settled in a new location, building new landforms like beaches and dunes.
UrbanisationThe growth of cities and towns as more people move from rural areas to urban ones.
StakeholderA person or group with an interest in how a piece of land is used or changed.
CountryFor Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, land as identity and kin, not just a place - cared for, not owned.
SustainabilityUsing resources in a way that meets present needs without harming the future.
Real-world case study to revise with

Mount Keira / Djeera and the Illawarra escarpment

This case study isn't from your textbook. It's here so you can practise the same ideas against a real NSW location, instead of just the textbook's own examples. Read each stage alongside the chapter shown in brackets, and feel free to use Mount Keira as your own case study in exam answers - linking what you've learnt to a real example like this helps solidify your understanding of the content.

  1. An introduction to the Illawarra - the geomorphic processes that formed the coastal and escarpment landscape (Ch.1).
  2. The first people of the Illawarra - the aesthetic, cultural, spiritual and economic value of Mount Keira / Djeera to the Dharawal people (Ch.2).
  3. Competing interests - modern uses of the mountain and the conflicts between different groups' values, compared against Queenstown, Cairns and Hobart (Ch.2-3).
  4. The Illawarra is changing - a timeline of use since European colonisation, nicknamed "Red gold" (coal), "Black gold", "Adrenalin" (mountain biking) and "The future?" (Ch.3).
  5. Managing the escarpment - Dharawal management practices compared with the modern bodies responsible for the mountain today (Ch.4).
  6. Geomorphic hazards: landslides - the real 2012 Mount Keira landslide, used the same way the textbook uses Japan 2011 (Ch.5).

Good for students who want a local, close-to-home example to compare against the textbook's own case studies.

Learn about Dharawal Country - on Djeera (Mount Keira) as a significant women's mountain, linked to the Five Sisters dreaming story.

Key ideas to revise

Natural processes

  • Weathering - rock breaks down in place (water, wind, temperature).
  • Erosion - rock or soil is picked up and moved (rivers, waves, wind).
  • Deposition - material is dropped and settled, building new landforms.
  • Coastal example: weather the cliff → break off chunks → carry along the coast → deposit as a beach.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Human processes

  • Four big ones: farming, urbanisation, mining, tourism.
  • Human change is often far faster than natural change - a mine can remove a hill in months.
  • Weigh up: jobs, infrastructure, food security against pollution, habitat loss, soil degradation.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Perspectives on the land

  • Stakeholders see the same landscape differently: residents, tourists, environmental groups, government.
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective: land is Country - identity, not ownership - cared for through cultural burning, seasonal knowledge, totemic protection.
  • Modern conservation instead uses national parks, scientific monitoring and laws.
  • Land-use decision: identify stakeholders → consult perspectives → weigh pros and cons → decide.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Evaluating change

  • Judge every change against three pillars: environmental, economic, social. Rarely all good or all bad.
  • Case study to know: the Snowy Mountains Scheme - jobs and cheap power versus flooded valleys and displaced farming communities.
  • Method: identify the change → gather evidence → analyse all three impacts → judge whether it's sustainable.
Mrs Perkins - from the iLearn Landscapes and Landforms lessons

Writing in geography

  • Describing a place: Location → Landscape.
  • Explaining a process: Process → Cause → Impact (e.g. "Wave erosion is shaping the coastline because powerful waves wear away limestone cliffs, causing sections to collapse into stacks.")
  • Writing a perspective fairly: Group → Viewpoint → Reason.
  • Report structure: Introduction → Body paragraphs → Perspectives → Conclusion (a judgement, not just a summary).
